FAQs

What is Swiss machining, and how does it differ from standard CNC turning?

Swiss machining, also called Swiss-type or Swiss screw machining, uses a sliding headstock and guide bushing to support bar stock close to the cutting tool. This reduces deflection when machining small, slender, or complex parts. Live tooling combines turning, milling, drilling, and threading in one setup. Conventional CNC turning generally leaves a longer unsupported section of the workpiece, which can make slender parts more prone to deflection.

What tolerances are achievable with Swiss machining?

The capability table gives reference tolerance levels of ±0.1 mm for general features, ±0.005 mm for precision features, and ±0.002 mm for selected critical features. Achievability depends on material, geometry, feature size, and inspection requirements. We confirm the applicable tolerances during drawing review rather than applying one tolerance to every part.

What are the typical part size ranges for Swiss machining?

Typical Swiss-machined parts have diameters around 1–32 mm. Micro Swiss machining addresses smaller diameters and finer features. Maximum length, minimum diameter, and feature size depend on the material, workholding, and geometry; send your drawing so we can confirm the machining and inspection approach.

Is Swiss machining suitable for prototypes, small batches, or high-volume production?

Yes. Swiss machining works for prototypes, short lead-time jobs, small batches, and high-volume production. Bar feeders, live tooling, sub-spindles, and automation support consistent, cost-effective medium-to-large batches.

What operations or capabilities are included in Swiss machining services?

Capabilities include multi-axis turning, milling, drilling, boring, threading, slotting, knurling, grooving, tapping, C-axis indexing, live tooling, and sub-spindle front/back operations in one setup. Secondary finishing, plating, heat treatment, or additional operations are planned according to the part drawing.

How does micro Swiss machining differ from Swiss screw machining?

Micro Swiss machining uses the same sliding-headstock and guide-bushing process as Swiss screw machining, with a focus on small diameters and fine features such as miniature threads, grooves, and cross-holes. Typical parts include micro pins, shafts, medical screws, dental components, and miniature connectors for medical devices, electronics, precision instruments, and miniature mechanisms. These parts require particular attention to tooling, burr control, handling, and measurement. Feasibility and tolerances are reviewed for the chosen material and geometry.

What minimum diameters and micro-features can you machine?

Minimum turned diameters, miniature threads, cross-holes, slots, and grooves depend on material, feature depth, length-to-diameter ratio, and the required tolerance and finish. Share a drawing identifying critical dimensions, surface finish, deburring, and inspection requirements. We review tooling and measurement access before confirming feasibility and a production quote.

How are chips, dimensional variation, and surface finish controlled?

Swiss screw machining can produce long chips in ductile materials. Process planning considers chip evacuation, coolant delivery, and tool geometry to reduce tangling and surface scratches. Guide-bushing fit, bar-stock consistency, collet support, temperature, and tool wear also affect dimensions. Speeds, feeds, and tool condition are reviewed to manage chatter, with inspection checks matched to the drawing and production run.

What factors affect lead times, costs, or quoting for Swiss machining?

Lead times and costs depend on CAD drawings, material specifications, tolerances, surface treatments, quantity, delivery needs, inspection requirements, and certifications such as ISO 9001 or AS9100.
